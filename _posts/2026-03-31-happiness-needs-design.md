> "The human eye can perceive millions of colors. Yet we can create this wide range from just three primary colors: red, yellow, and blue. In the same way, we can think about the full range of happiness by looking at three primary kinds: the happiness of pleasure, the happiness of grace, and the happiness of excellence."
> — Eric Greitens, *Resilience*

Greitens uses color as an analogy for happiness. I'd name the three primaries slightly differently — pleasure, satisfaction, and meaning — but the point holds. People who are flourishing usually have all three in their lives.

You can't trade one away and compensate with the others. You can temporarily lower the dose of pleasure because you're fighting for something meaningful — studying for exams, building a career, raising small children. But the balance needs to come back. Neglect one pillar for too long, and the whole structure becomes unstable. Not dramatically, not overnight. Slowly, quietly, in a way you don't notice until you're already off.

Pleasure probably can't give you meaning on its own. But losing pleasure and satisfaction for long enough can make you completely unable to search for meaning. The pillars don't compete — they support each other.

I know this because I lived it.


For fifteen years of my young life, I played tennis heavily. When I was really young, I loved to play football with friends. After school, my brother and I often played basketball. Almost every afternoon and evening was spent playing sports. In secondary school, I added volleyball and handball. Almost all holidays as an adolescent I spent at tennis camps.

And here's the thing — I had no conscious conviction that I loved it. I never told myself "sport is my passion" or "this is what makes me happy." I just did it, and I was happy without thinking about it. The pleasure was so natural it was invisible.


In high school, the frequency of playing dropped drastically. But it seemed like a normal sequence of life. You need to study more, focus on education. University was even harsher — I stopped practicing sport entirely. A lot of learning, partying, just university life.

But everything seemed fine. I traded sports for literature, for intellectual life. I read constantly, and I liked it — but it was a different kind of satisfaction. I was slowly losing one color without noticing.

Having turned twenty-five, I started working. Several years later, family life took over. Spare time evaporated. And somewhere in this stretch — I can't point to the exact moment — my mental state started to erode. I couldn't tell why. Probably overworked. New chores and responsibilities hit me harder than I expected.

But the real issue wasn't workload. It was that nothing gave me pure joy anymore. I had nothing I could point to and say: this is fun. I liked resting. I liked learning. But enjoyment — the raw, physical, thoughtless kind — was gone.


I started to wonder if something was broken in me. Maybe depression, maybe anhedonia. My brain just didn't produce the feeling anymore. For a long time, I genuinely believed the problem was internal — some wiring that had gone wrong.

And this is where it gets dangerous. People who are depressed hate hearing "just exercise, go outside, it will help." And at that moment, they're right to hate it. When you're already in the dark, that advice is useless. It's too late.

That's exactly why prevention matters. You need to maintain the things that bring you joy *before* you lose them entirely. The slide from "I don't have time for fun" to "I can't feel fun" is longer than you think, but once you've crossed it, the way back is brutal. You don't just need to find joy again — you need to believe it's still possible for you.


About a year ago, I started playing padel. And something shifted.

It wasn't dramatic. I just noticed, after a session, that my brain felt different. An hour and a half of complete concentration, separated from the world and everyday problems. Pure flow. And afterward — I just felt good. Simply, physically good. I hadn't felt that in a long time.

My brain wasn't broken. I could still feel joy. But joy wasn't going to come to me — I had to go and find it. I had to experiment, try things, design the conditions where enjoyment could happen. Padel worked. Something else might work too. The point wasn't the specific activity. The point was that I had some control over my own happiness, but only if I was willing to be active about it.

And then the dots connected. The happiness of my childhood wasn't some magical property of being young. Of course many things contributed — friends, fewer responsibilities, the lightness of not yet carrying adult weight. But sport was a huge part of it. Physical effort, struggle, improvement, flow — all happening naturally, every single day, for fifteen years. And then I spent over a decade without it, wondering why the picture of my life looked flat.


Back in high school and university, I thought I needed to invest in meaning. Find my orientation in life. And of course that matters — deeply. But I overestimated what meaning alone could do. I believed that happiness could come from inside. That deep thinking, beautiful ideas, intellectual growth would be enough. I fought for meaning and dismissed pleasure as a lower category.

But pleasure isn't a lower category. It's a primary color. And I was trying to paint my life using only one.
