### How Nikola Tesla Built Himself

## A Horse, or a Staircase

When Nikola Tesla was seven, his older brother Daniel died.

The circumstances were never settled. Some accounts say a horse kicked him. Others — including different versions Tesla himself told later in life — say he fell down a staircase, possibly pushed by Nikola.

Whichever version is true, the boy who lived carried it.

To console his parents, the seven-year-old made a private promise: he would change. He would become worth the brother he had lost.

The discipline came. So did something else, as his biographer Margaret Cheney later wrote:

> "He would be more spartan, more studious than other boys, more generous, and in every way superior. And it was while denying himself and repressing natural impulses, he later believed, that he began to develop his strange compulsions." [1]

The compulsions arrived around the same time. Counting the cubic content of his soup plate before he could eat it. Counting steps as he walked. An aversion to earrings on women so visceral he could barely look at them.

The eccentricity had a power of its own. Tesla would spend the rest of his life learning how to use it.

## Inheritance and Appetite

Nikola Tesla was born on the night of July 9/10, 1856, in Smiljan — a village in what was then the Austrian Empire, now Croatia.

His father, the Reverend Milutin Tesla, was an Orthodox Serbian priest. He wrote poetry in his spare time, planned a clergy career for his son, and treated parenthood as a kind of cognitive workout. Daily drills: guess one another's thoughts. Find the defects of form in a sentence. Repeat long passages from memory. Perform calculations.

His mother never learned to read. The eldest of seven, she had been pulled out of school as a child to take over the household when her own mother went blind. What she had instead was a memory that would not let her go — she could recite whole volumes of Serbian and European poetry verbatim. She built her own household tools: looms, churns, devices her neighbors had never seen.

Tesla believed he had inherited his memory and his inventive instincts from her.

He started tinkering young. At five, he built a small waterwheel — smooth, bladeless, unlike anything on the local creeks. Decades later, when he designed his bladeless turbine, he would remember that wheel.

Most of his early experiments failed.

He climbed onto the barn roof clutching the family umbrella, hyperventilating until he felt light enough to fly, and plunged. His mother carried him to bed, unconscious. He took his grandfather's clocks apart and could never reassemble them: "In the former operation," he later wrote, "I was always successful but often failed in the latter." [2] Thirty years passed before he tried clockwork again.

At eight, the reading began.

His father forbade candles, fearing he would ruin his eyes. Nikola made his own. He stuffed rags in the keyhole and door cracks and read until his mother began her morning rounds at dawn.

Then he found *Abafi*, a Hungarian novel. Tesla said it "awakened my dormant powers of will" [3] — and from it, he traced the rigorous discipline that would make him an inventor.

He was eight years old, and already at work on himself.

## The Strange Wiring

Discipline and self-control are easy to understand. The rest of Tesla is not.

From his earliest memory, he saw flashes of light. Images of objects appeared in his mind so vividly that the line between picture and presence dissolved. The flashes tormented him.

To escape them, he began conjuring imaginary worlds of his own. Every night, before sleep, he set out on what he later called make-believe journeys — new places, new cities, new countries. He lived there. He met people and made friends. The journeys, he wrote, "were just as dear to me as those in actual life, and not a bit less intense in their manifestations." [4]

He kept doing it until he was seventeen.

Then, when his thoughts turned to invention, he discovered that something had been built. He could picture machines in his mind so completely that he needed no drawings, no models, no experiments. He could redesign a part, run it, watch it fail, redesign again — all in his head. By the time he sat down to build, the invention was already finished.

The same faculty could deceive him for years. Around age ten, he built a sealed cylinder he was sure proved continuous motion through air pressure, and rode in it daily inside his head — "a vehicle of comfort and luxury as might have befitted King Solomon" — before discovering the slight rotation came from a leak. [6]

Was this gift or training? Both — and as Cheney put it, you can't separate them:

> "Tesla's childhood development is confusing because he enhanced his native talent with such rigorous mental discipline that it is impossible to separate the innate gifts from the acquired." [5]

Eidetic memory of the kind Tesla showed — memorizing a page of type at a glance, holding the precise relationships of myriad patterns in his head — typically begins to wane in adolescence. Tesla's didn't. Whether that was the gift surviving, or the training holding it open, no one could say.

His senses were sharper than other people's. Acute discomfort from the smell of camphor. The ability to hear a watch ticking three rooms away.

The mind that heard a watch three rooms away was the mind that built machines in its head.

## An Impossible Idea

At the gymnasium, math came easily. Languages too. He hated drawing classes — by the time he picked up a pencil, the drawing was already finished in his head.

He wanted to be an engineer. His father, the priest, had other plans.

At seventeen, Tesla nearly died of cholera. From his sickbed, he extracted a promise: if he survived, he could study engineering. His father agreed.

Tesla recovered.

In 1875 he enrolled at the Graz Polytechnic on a fellowship from the Austro-Hungarian military frontier. He had no money worries and a great deal of ambition. He crammed two years' worth of coursework into one. He studied physics under Professor Poeschl, whose demonstrations fascinated him.

A direct-current Gramme machine arrived in the lab from Paris — a device that worked as both motor and dynamo. Tesla watched it run. The commutator sparked badly. He told Poeschl, brashly, that the machine could be improved by getting rid of the commutator and using alternating current.

> "Mr. Tesla may accomplish great things," Poeschl replied, "but he will never do this. It would be equivalent to converting a steadily pulling force, like that of gravity, into a rotary effort. It is a perpetual motion machine, an impossible idea." [7]

Tesla had no idea how to do it either. But once the problem was lodged in his head, he could not put it down.

The next year, the military frontier was abolished. The fellowship vanished. Tesla finished what he could, ran out of money, and prepared to leave.

Out of school, he gambled. He drank. He smoked. He did not believe in free will — he would later write of humans as automatons — and he stopped all three anyway.

What he could not stop was the problem in his head. He would carry it for six years.

## Six Years Later

In 1881, Tesla was twenty-five, working at a telegraph office in Budapest. He had been carrying the AC problem for six years, and still had no idea how to solve it.

Then he started to come apart.

His pulse swung between normal and 250 beats per minute. The ground trembled under his feet — actually trembled, in his perception, all the time. He could hear a watch ticking three rooms away. They put runner cushions under his bed so he could sleep. Doctors had no name for what was happening.

His friend Szigety pulled him outside. They walked. Szigety convinced him that exercise would help, and Tesla — who had always preferred his own head to his own body — let himself be led into the open air.

One evening in 1882, they were walking in a park at sunset. Tesla had been reciting Faust. He came to a passage about the setting sun:

> *The glow retreats, done in the day of toil;
> It yonder hastes, new fields of life exploring;
> Ah, that no wing can lift me from the soil,
> Upon its track to follow, follow soaring!*

Then, in his own words: "the idea came like a flash of lightning, and in an instant the truth was revealed." [8]

His arms froze in midair. Szigety, alarmed, tried to lead him to a bench. Tesla refused to sit until he had found a stick. Then he bent over the dirt at the edge of the path and began to draw a diagram in the dust.

"See my motor here," he said. "Watch me reverse it."

The diagram was the rotating magnetic field — alternating currents out of phase with each other, producing a magnetic whirlwind that needed no commutator. Not a new motor. An entire new system. Six years later, Tesla would present that same diagram at the American Institute of Electrical Engineers in New York. It would change the world.

"Ideas came in an uninterrupted stream," Tesla later said of the months that followed, "and the only difficulty I had was to hold them fast." [9]

It looks like a eureka moment.

But the flash had been six years in the making — and underneath those six, longer. The night candles. The imaginary worlds. The bladeless waterwheel. The discipline he had chosen at seven. What lit up in the park between Faust and a stick was the surface of a much older, quieter thing.

## America

Tesla arrived in New York in 1884 with four cents in his pocket, drawings of a flying machine, and a letter of recommendation to Edison.

He was twenty-eight. The inventor he would be was already finished.

Edison hired him. They worked side by side for nearly a year, sometimes through two or three nights without sleep. They disliked each other anyway.

Their minds did not work the same way. Edison was a brute-force troubleshooter who ran experiments by the thousand and kept what worked. Tesla worked the opposite direction — he wanted to understand the principle first, in his head, completely, before touching a wire. He had been doing it that way since the imaginary worlds.

Tesla, on Edison:

> "If Edison had a needle to find in a haystack, he would proceed at once with the diligence of the bee to examine straw after straw until he found the object of his search. I was a sorry witness of such doings, knowing that a little theory and calculation would have saved him ninety percent of his labor." [10]

Edison, on Tesla, was kinder and more cutting at once: "the poet of science." Magnificent. Impractical. Both were probably right.

They split within a year. Edison was committed to direct current (DC); Tesla had brought alternating current (AC) with him from a park in Budapest. Eventually AC won — through Westinghouse, through patents Tesla sold cheaply rather than collect royalties on, through being right. Every electrical grid on Earth carries what he drew that evening in the park. But by the time the world caught up, Tesla was already moving on.

He always was. Once he understood an idea fully, his interest in it began to wane. He did not protect his inventions. He did not push for credit. Marconi read Tesla's radio lectures and never acknowledged him; the cosmic-ray and electron-microscope ideas embedded in his early lamps were absorbed into other people's Nobel work.

He was a thinker and an inventor. He was not a marketer.

## What He Carried

Tesla's mind was built. The curiosity, the willingness to learn, the appetite to experiment, the refusal to set down a problem until he understood it — these were the foundations of everything that came later.

They were visible at five, when he built the bladeless waterwheel. They sharpened at seven, in the dark of his grief over Daniel, when he made a private promise to be more spartan, more studious, more generous than other boys. They were applied at eight, when he made his own candles to keep reading. They survived at ten, when the perpetual-motion cylinder fooled him for years and he kept building anyway. They cross-fed for the next twenty: curiosity fed reading, reading fed curiosity; discipline fed ambition, ambition fed discipline; visualization fed invention, invention sharpened visualization. By the time he stood in the Budapest park between Faust and a stick, what lit up was the surface of all of it.

These traits were not strange. They are on the table for anyone.

The story does not end here. Tesla had another sixty years — radio he never got credit for, Wardenclyffe, the long decline, a death forgotten in 1943. But the inventor was already finished, and what made him was already in place.

Two readings of Tesla are defensible from the same facts. One: some minds are simply given. Two: thirty years of obsessive preparation makes the gift look like one. The life will not let us pick.

But the life will also not let us pretend the preparation was optional. The flashes were his. The visualization that began before he could have trained it was his. The eidetic memory that did not wane was his. But the night candles, the imaginary worlds carried until seventeen, the cylinder that fooled him for years, the six years of carrying Poeschl's problem — those were on the table for anyone willing to spend them.

The flash is his. The preparation is on the table for anyone.
