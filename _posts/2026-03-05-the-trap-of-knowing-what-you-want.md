## The Modern Promise

For most of human history, the question "what do you want from life?" didn't make much sense. You were born into a role — farmer, craftsman, wife of a farmer, wife of a craftsman. Your path was decided before you could walk. Freedom, in the modern sense of designing your own life, simply wasn't on the table.

We've changed that. Over the last few centuries, we've built societies where individuals can choose their careers, their partners, their values, their identities. This is a genuine achievement — arguably the central achievement of modernity. More people today have more options than any generation before them.

But this freedom comes with a hidden burden. To choose a path, you must first know what you want. And knowing what you want turns out to be far harder than it looks.

We all carry mental models of the good life — pictures of what we desire, what would make us fulfilled, what we should pursue. The trouble isn't that we lack these models. We have them. We can even implement them perfectly. And still fail.

Why? Because we have no way to tell — on our own, from inside our own head — whether our model has any real connection to the life it's supposed to represent.

## Oblomov

Goncharov's *Oblomov* is a story about a man who had everything he needed to flourish — intelligence, education, wealth, kind heart, loyal friends — and still perished. I've written about him before [1], and I keep coming back because his failure is so instructive. It wasn't an execution error.

Oblomov knew exactly what he wanted. His mental model of the ideal life was vivid and specific — drawn from Oblomovka, the estate where he grew up. A place of warmth, rhythm, stillness. Seasonal routines, familiar faces, no disruption. He didn't want abstract "happiness." He wanted *that* — the feeling of Oblomovka — transplanted into his adult life.

And in a sense, he got it. He found Agafya — a woman who provided exactly the domestic peace he was looking for. Warm meals, quiet days, no demands. The model, implemented.

He died on that sofa. "Just like a clock that has stopped because it has not been wound up." [1]

The usual reading is that Oblomov failed at execution — he was too lazy to act. But I think the failure happened earlier. His mental model itself was flawed. Not because Oblomovka was a bad place, but because what made Oblomovka work couldn't be captured in a private picture of warmth and safety. Oblomovka was a shared practice — a whole community of relationships, rituals, roles, obligations. Oblomov extracted the sensation and left behind the structure. He carried a private feeling and called it a plan for life.

The question is: why can't that work?

## Why Private Models Fail

We tend to think the process works like this: you look inside yourself, figure out what you want, build a mental model of the good life, and then go pursue it. "I want security." "I want freedom." "I want meaningful work." These feel clear and actionable. If things go wrong, we assume either the execution failed or the model was imprecise — we just need to try harder, think deeper, dig into what we *really* want.

But what if the problem starts earlier? Before execution, before precision — at the very project of sitting alone and building a private representation of "what I want"?

A mental model does two things at once. It represents reality — "this is what the good life looks like." And it prescribes action — "therefore I should do X." It's both a picture and a rule. Wittgenstein showed that both dimensions break down when they stay private.

Take the picture first. Your model of the good life is built from private experience — memories, feelings, impressions. But your private experience alone doesn't determine what your model actually means in practice. Oblomov's picture of Oblomovka felt perfectly clear to him. Warm, stable, safe. But "warm and stable" doesn't tell you whether to marry Olga or settle with Agafya, whether to reform your estate or ignore it, whether to stay in bed or get up. The same picture is compatible with opposite lives. Wittgenstein made this point about private sensations [2]: if only you can see what's inside your box, then the thing inside plays no role in determining what your words actually mean. A private picture, no matter how vivid, can't anchor its own interpretation.

Now take the rule. "I want security, so I should avoid risk." This feels like clear guidance. But Wittgenstein's rule-following paradox [2] shows it isn't: *"No course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule."* "Avoid risk" can mean staying home, or getting a stable job, or marrying someone safe, or never marrying at all. Every one of these can be justified as following the same rule. A rule without external checks — without someone or something that can say "no, that's not what it means" — isn't really a rule. It's a feeling dressed up as guidance.

This isn't about imprecision. Imprecision is a quantitative problem — fixable with more data, more reflection. What Wittgenstein identifies is structural. A private model can't establish its own criteria of correctness. It feels determinate from the inside. It isn't. And no amount of private refinement makes it so.

Oblomov refined his model for years. In his head, it was perfectly clear. And the more privately he refined it, the less it could do for him.

## What Now

Oblomov's retreat from correction wasn't just a 19th-century Russian problem. We've done the same thing at cultural scale.

Modern liberal societies have gradually stripped communities of the authority to correct. A community that never says "no" functions the same way Agafya did for Oblomov — it accommodates, it affirms, but it provides no more real guidance than solitude does. Everything goes if it's someone's choice. Any attempt to suggest that some paths lead nowhere is dismissed as judgmental.

In Wittgenstein's terms, when "whatever seems right to you is right," there's no difference between following your model and merely thinking you follow it. The freedom to live as you want, without communal correction, doesn't liberate your mental model. It makes it structurally incapable of guiding you.

Edmund Burke saw a version of this two centuries ago [3]. He argued that individuals would do better to "avail themselves of the general bank and capital of nations and of ages" rather than rely on their own "private stock of reason." Traditions, however imperfect, encode the accumulated trial and error of generations — more knowledge than any one person can derive from first principles. Burke was right about the limitation, even if his answer was incomplete. Tradition encodes oppression alongside wisdom, and he gave no criterion for telling the two apart.

So what does work? Wittgenstein's answer is precise: shared practice. A rule becomes a real rule only when embedded in communal criteria of correctness — in what he called "forms of life." Not tradition for tradition's sake, but the ongoing, living practice of people who can say to each other: "No, that's not what it means."

The conclusion isn't "don't build mental models." You can't avoid it — we all start with a picture of what we want. The conclusion is that a private model — one that has never been exposed to friction, to disagreement, to the world pushing back — isn't yet a model. It's a private impression. And private impressions can't guide you, no matter how vivid they feel.

We live in a world where we can choose our path. That freedom is real, and nothing in this essay argues against it. But choosing freely and choosing well are not the same thing. The freedom to design your own life doesn't mean you can tell — privately, from the inside — whether your design will work. It's usually wiser to test your model against shared practice, to watch what happens when it meets reality, than to trust your heart alone and build from scratch. Not because your instincts are worthless. But because a model that only you can see is a model that no one — including you — can correct.

The corrective isn't to stop thinking. It's to stop thinking alone — and to stop being too certain you know what's best for you.
